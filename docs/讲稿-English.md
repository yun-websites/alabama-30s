# Presentation Script (English)

Hello everyone. Today I am introducing this display board for the introduction to _To Kill a Mockingbird_. The main purpose of the board is not to retell the novel itself, but to place it in the real historical context of 1930s Alabama. That context matters, because the pressures, limits, and prejudices in the story did not appear out of nowhere. They came from the world people were living in.

The opening section is the cover. It uses images of rural Alabama, a schoolhouse, and cotton fields to create a sense of moving from history into literature. The title, “A teenager’s survival guide,” is meant to push against any romantic idea of youth. In the years of the Great Depression, segregation, and economic strain, everyday life for teenagers was often shaped by survival first.

Next is the “day in fragments” section. We divide the day into moments before class, at school, after school, and in the evening so that students can see how ordinary life was structured. For some young people, the day started with work at home before school. For others, schooling meant long walks, short terms, worn books, or irregular attendance. This section also highlights how unequal education was, especially under segregation.

The “survival guide” section contains five files: clothing, school, free time, family life, and rules. Each one offers a different way to read the novel. Clothing shows the importance of repairing and reusing what people had. School shows that learning was limited by money, distance, and unequal systems. Free time shows that recreation was usually local and inexpensive. Family life shows how children often carried real responsibility. And the rules section reminds us that customs and laws both shaped behavior, but they were not always fair.

The “hard times” panel focuses on the impact of the Great Depression in Alabama. The numbers here are not included just to sound official. They show that poverty, job loss, and falling incomes were concrete historical realities. Cotton prices dropped sharply, school systems struggled to pay teachers, and many children attended school for only part of the year. Those conditions help explain the tension and hardship that appear in the novel’s world.

The fact file gives seven key evidence points in a compact format. It is meant to be a quick reference while reading, so students can keep the most important historical details in mind without getting lost in the volume of information.

Finally, the closing section offers three better questions to carry into the novel. Instead of only asking what happens, we should ask who has enough money, time, or safety to make a real choice; which rules are just customs and which are backed by law or fear; and how a character’s family, schooling, race, or place in town affects how others judge them.

In short, this display board is trying to do one thing: put history back in front of the novel before we read it. That way, we are not just reading a story. We are reading a story shaped by a particular time and place. Thank you.
